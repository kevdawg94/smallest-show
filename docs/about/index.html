About the project

Nobody keeps count of America's independent cinemas

Smallest Show started as a widget for one person's Friday nights. To make it work beyond his own four theaters, we had to find every independent cinema in the country, and found that no one keeps a list. Other countries count their cinemas because they fund them. The United States does neither.

The list nobody keeps

The people who know America's art houses best say it plainly:

“There is no single entity that maintains a complete dataset of all independent art house exhibitors.”

Art House Convergence, 2025 Landscape Survey1

The counts that do exist disagree by an order of magnitude, because each one counts something different:

CountWhat it counts
358Independent art-house exhibitors in North America, estimated by the art houses' own association1
~1,600Movie theater operators given federal pandemic relief (Shuttered Venue grants), 2021–222
4,006Cinema businesses with paid staff, chains included (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024)3
~5,000Screens run by the Independent Cinema Alliance's 180 or so member companies, in the US and Canada4
2,730Open independent cinemas found by Smallest Show's survey, house by house

We made the last one ourselves, and it's still growing: county by county, we checked every cinema a directory, a map or a listing names, open or closed, independent or chain, and whether its schedule can be read. We found 2,730 open independent cinemas, and the widget reads 1,783 of them, 65%. The rest are missing for reasons we write down:

  • 1,783read by the widget
  • 745post their times where the widget can't read them yet
  • 202seasonal, closed part of the year

As of November 5, 2026. Along the way the survey set aside more than 2,200 listed cinemas that have closed and about 1,600 that belong to chains.

What counts as independent. A cinema not owned by a national chain. A circuit counts as a chain when investors own it (a public company, private equity, a conglomerate or another exhibitor), when it runs houses in ten or more states, or when it runs more than 50. A family-, founder-, locally-, employee- or nonprofit-owned circuit of 50 or fewer counts house by house, like any independent, and an art house is never a chain, whoever runs it.

Other countries count, because they pay

Where a government funds cinemas, it needs a test for who qualifies, and the test needs a record of what each screen shows. So the countries that support their art houses know exactly where those houses are and what they play.

  • France

    Every cinema reports each screening's admissions and receipts to the national film agency, the CNC, every week, by law.5 In 2026 it classified 1,357 cinemas and travelling circuits Art et Essai and paid them about €19.6 million, scaled to the share of their screenings that went to recommended films.6

  • Germany

    The Film Act requires every cinema that sells tickets to report admissions and revenue for each film on each screen to the national film board every month.7 A federal premium, Liebling Kino, pays cinemas for what they choose to show: its first round paid €6.7 million to 464 of them.8

  • United Kingdom

    The BFI's Film Audience Network gets £10.8 million of National Lottery money for 2026–29 to build audiences for independent film across the country.9

  • Europe

    Europa Cinemas, the EU-backed network of independent cinemas, counts 1,290 members in 39 countries. To keep its support, each one reports its screenings and admissions film by film.10

  • South Korea and Brazil

    Korea's cinemas send every ticket sold to the national film council's network, compulsory since 2010.11 Brazil's cinemas report every screening each day, and its film agency publishes each one's attendance as open data.12

  • United States

    No federal program pays a cinema for what it shows, and no public body keeps count of the independents.

The US funds none of it

American support for the arts runs mostly through tax deductions for private gifts. In 2004 the National Endowment for the Arts called itself “less than 1 percent of total arts philanthropy in the U.S.,” and admitted: “No one—not even the NEA—has exact statistics on American cultural institutions.”13 Its whole budget, for every art form in the country, was $207 million in 2026, and the White House's budget for 2027 proposes closing it.14

The one federal program that reached cinemas at scale was pandemic relief, which kept theaters alive without asking what they showed.2 Abroad, money leads to a test and the test to a record. In the US the chain breaks at its first link, so nobody has a reason to count.

What Smallest Show does about it

It counts. Every three hours through the day, it reads the public schedules of every independent cinema it can, politely and under its own name (what we read, and how often), and puts tonight's shows on your home screen. Behind the widget, it keeps every read, dated: a week-by-week record of what America's independent screens actually show. As far as we can find, no one else keeps such a record.

The survey is the denominator: each house it finds is filed with what keeps it out, so the coverage above can be checked and can grow. A theater the widget can't read yet is one to win back by asking, not a dead end. If you run one, tell us, and if there's a cinema you go to that's missing, ask on GitHub.

Made by Kevin Celustka for his own Friday nights, and named for The Smallest Show on Earth (1957), where a young couple inherit a run-down cinema between two railway bridges.

No accounts, no ads, no tracking: the widget reads a public file. The showtimes are the theaters'; we only point to them.
